# Economic conditions and the changing scale and organisation of female sex work in Pakistan: a multi-period ecological study

Running title: Economic conditions and female sex work in Pakistan

Article type: Original research

## Abstract

### Objectives

To determine whether economic conditions were associated with the density, population size, growth, and organisation of mapped female sex work across Pakistan's complete published surveillance series.

### Methods

We conducted a retrospective ecological study of 49 city-round observations from female sex work mapping and surveillance in 2006-07, 2011, 2014, and 2016-17. City outcomes were matched to district urban indicators and national economic conditions. The primary analysis correlated recent household economic deterioration with mapped density across 15 cities in 2011. Secondary analyses examined absolute population, work setting, common-city change, and 32 round-level economic-marker correlations using permutation or exact inference and false-discovery-rate adjustment.

### Results

Recent deterioration was weakly inversely associated with density in 2011 (Spearman rho=-0.138; bootstrap 95% CI -0.597 to 0.372; permutation p=0.620), but positively associated with absolute mapped population (rho=0.657) and kothikhana share (rho=0.618). All-area deterioration and kothikhana share was the only association surviving adjustment across 54 cross-sectional tests (rho=0.771; q=0.041). During economic deterioration, the common ten-city mapped population increased 66.3%, while median home-based share rose 18.3 percentage points and street-based share fell 24.2 points. In continuous panels, annualised growth slowed from 9.65% to 5.30% in four cities and from 14.39% to 0.87% in six cities as conditions improved. None of 32 round-level macroeconomic correlations survived exact inference and multiplicity adjustment.

### Conclusions

Pakistan's published series contains an economic signal, but density alone does not capture it. Deterioration coincided with rapid mapped-population growth and organisational change; improvement coincided with marked growth deceleration toward a plateau. The pattern is consistent with reduced net entry during recovery alongside barriers to exit, although individual transitions were not measured.

## Key messages

### What is already known on this topic

Economic need is a common reason for entering and continuing sex work, but Pakistani evidence has not previously linked repeated mapping estimates to contemporaneous economic conditions.

### What this study adds

Across Pakistan's complete published surveillance series, worsening conditions coincided with faster mapped-population growth and organisational change, while improving conditions coincided with a pronounced slowdown toward a plateau; cross-sectional density was not the informative marker.

### How this study might affect research, practice or policy

HIV programmes should monitor absolute population, growth, and work organisation alongside density and should adapt outreach when economic change shifts sex work into less visible or intermediary-linked settings.

Keywords: female sex workers; HIV surveillance; economic conditions; Pakistan; population size estimation

## Introduction

Pakistan's HIV epidemic is expanding while diagnosis, treatment, and viral suppression remain limited. In 2024, an estimated 350,000 people were living with HIV and 48,000 acquired HIV, three times the estimated number of new infections in 2010. [1] National adult prevalence remains low, but surveillance shows a concentrated and geographically uneven epidemic; HIV prevalence among female sex workers was 2.2% in 2016-17, with substantially higher prevalence in some cities. [2] Pakistan's national strategy consequently treats key-population size estimates and city surveillance as essential for prevention planning. [3]

Female sex work in Pakistan includes street-, home-, brothel-, kothikhana-, and cellphone-mediated arrangements. [4] Population size, concentration, work setting, and client volume vary markedly between cities, and repeated surveillance has documented growth and organisational change. [5,6] Movement into homes or mediated networks can alter both economic exposure and the reach of HIV services.

Economic conditions may affect entry, continuation, demand, and work setting in different directions. In Lahore, 91% of surveyed participants cited financial need as a reason for entering sex work, 99% cited economic survival as a reason for continuing, and median duration in sex work was seven years. [7] During Zimbabwe's economic collapse, sexual-economic exchange became more private, informal, and commodity based, while loss of wages also reduced client demand; activity later increased with recovery. [8,9] Conversely, economic expansion in Zambian mining cities and stronger outside-employment incentives in Cambodia were associated with less transactional sex or sex work. [10,11]

These mechanisms imply that density, absolute population, organisation, and growth need not move together. Deterioration may increase economic pressure while reducing client spending; recovery may reduce new entry without rapidly reversing an accumulated population when exit is difficult. No previous study has combined Pakistan's complete published female sex work mapping series with matched local socioeconomic surveys and national economic indicators.

We examined whether economic conditions were associated with mapped female sex worker density, population size, growth, and organisation across Pakistani cities and surveillance rounds. The prespecified primary analysis tested whether recent household economic deterioration was positively associated with 2011 mapped density. Secondary analyses examined chronic deprivation, absolute population, work setting, network organisation, cross-round change, improved conditions, and national macroeconomic indicators.

## Methods

### Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective multi-period ecological study reported according to STROBE. [12] One observation represented one city in one surveillance round. The dataset contained 49 city-round observations: 12 in 2006-07, 15 in 2011, four in 2014, and 18 in 2016-17. Surveillance coverage and methods changed, so we analysed rounds separately and used common-city panels for longitudinal comparisons rather than pooling all observations.

Female sex work measures came from national surveillance Round II, Round IV, the 2014 Punjab survey, the 2015 mapping publication, and Round V. [2,13-16] The 2011 round was primary because it alone reported city-specific density per 1,000 adult men with broad coverage. Earlier rounds supplied population totals and work-setting counts; Round V supplied city totals but no comparable city density or typology table.

District socioeconomic indicators came from Pakistan Social and Living Standards Measurement Survey reports for 2006-07, 2010-11, and 2014-15. [17-19] The primary exposure was the urban percentage of households reporting that their economic situation was worse or much worse than one year earlier. Secondary 2011 measures were model-based urban poverty, female illiteracy, sanitation deprivation, and an equal-weight standardised chronic-deprivation index. [20] National GDP growth, inflation, per-capita income, and unemployment came from Pakistan Economic Surveys. [21-24]

### Eligibility, matching, and outcomes

We included official or peer-reviewed sources with extractable city measures and a defensible geographic and temporal match. Cities entered an analysis only when both required variables were available. Missing values were not imputed, unavailable density or typology was not coded as zero, and adult-male denominators were not reconstructed for rounds without published density. Round V's 18 printed city estimates summed to 71,315 whereas its printed grand total was 64,829; we retained city rows for common-city comparisons but did not use the inconsistent grand total.

Cities were linked through an explicit geographic crosswalk to district urban strata and graded as close, composite, district proxy, or problematic. Sensitivity analyses restricted geographic quality and excluded megacities. Outcomes were mapped density, absolute mapped population, work-setting shares, and network measures. Cross-round analyses used identical-city panels and kept national indicators at their true round level.

### Statistical analysis

The primary statistic was Spearman's correlation between 2011 urban household deterioration and mapped density. We calculated a two-sided 100,000-permutation p value and a 20,000-resample percentile bootstrap 95% confidence interval. Sensitivity analyses used Pearson correlation, alternative deprivation measures, leave-one-city-out estimates, geographic restrictions, log density, and ordinary least-squares models with HC3 robust standard errors. Linear, quadratic, and cubic models were compared by leave-one-city-out prediction error.

An exploratory atlas crossed six economic exposures with nine sex-work outcomes (54 correlations), with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. Separate analyses examined network structure, standardised two-exposure models, within-round typology associations, and city-level changes. Common-city panels compared combined totals and approximate annualised growth across deterioration and recovery. Thirty-two correlations paired four national indicators with eight round-level sex-work or survey markers; exact permutation inference and multiplicity adjustment preserved the true sample of three or four rounds.

Bias handling included within-round comparisons, explicit match grades, common-city panels, restricted samples, influence diagnostics, robust standard errors, permutation or exact inference, and multiplicity adjustment. Automated checks verified provenance, row uniqueness, expected city counts, positive totals, percentage ranges, typology reconciliation, and missingness. Analyses used Python, pandas, and NumPy; derived data and code are provided with the article.

### Ethics

Only publicly available aggregate reports and de-identified secondary data were used. There was no participant recruitment, contact, intervention, or access to identifiable records; ethical approval and individual informed consent were not required.

## Results

### Primary and cross-sectional results

The 49 observations represented 12 cities and 49,037 mapped female sex workers in 2006-07; 15 cities and 89,178 in 2011; four Punjab cities and 44,160 in 2014; and 18 Round V city rows summing to 71,315. All 15 primary cities had complete density, mapped population, and socioeconomic exposures.

Recent deterioration was weakly inversely associated with 2011 density (rho=-0.138; bootstrap 95% CI -0.597 to 0.372; permutation p=0.620). Pearson correlation was -0.196. All leave-one-city-out estimates remained negative (-0.264 to -0.031), while geographic restrictions yielded small estimates in either direction. Robust linear, adjusted, and log-density models were weak and imprecise. Polynomial models did not improve out-of-sample prediction over a constant-only model.

Recent deterioration was positively associated with absolute mapped population (rho=0.657; raw p=0.0078; q=0.069) and kothikhana share (rho=0.618; raw p=0.014; q=0.069), and inversely associated with cellphone-based share (rho=-0.515; raw p=0.050; q=0.167). All-area deterioration and kothikhana share was the only association surviving correction across 54 tests (rho=0.771; q=0.041). Chronic deprivation showed the opposite scale pattern: it was inversely associated with mapped total (rho=-0.700; q=0.054) but weakly positively associated with density (rho=0.315). Network and two-exposure model estimates were directionally consistent but imprecise.

### Deterioration, recovery, and organisation

Between 2006-07 and 2011, GDP growth fell from 7.0% to 2.4%, inflation rose from 7.9% to 14.0%, and median household deterioration increased 20.84 percentage points in ten common cities. Their combined mapped population increased from 47,062 to 78,241 (+66.25%; approximately 11.96% annually). Eight of ten cities increased. Median home-based share rose 18.32 percentage points, street-based share fell 24.16 points, and kothikhana share rose 6.99 points. City-level change correlations and within-round structural correlations were not stable, indicating a period pattern rather than a universal city ranking.

As national conditions improved, mapped totals did not immediately fall but growth slowed. Four common Punjab cities increased 16.77% from 2011 to 2014 (approximately 5.30% annually). Ten common cities increased only 3.36% through 2016-17 (0.60% annually); four increased, six decreased, and median city change was -1.94%. In strict continuous panels, annualised growth slowed from 9.65% to 5.30% in four cities and from 14.39% to 0.87% in six cities.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Pakistan's complete published surveillance series contains an economic signal, but not the simple positive density relationship initially tested. Recent deterioration marked larger cities and larger mapped populations, while chronic deprivation marked smaller cities with somewhat higher density. The clearest longitudinal pattern was rapid mapped-population growth and organisational change during deterioration followed by pronounced growth deceleration toward a plateau during recovery.

Density is a ratio and cannot represent all dimensions of a changing market. It may remain stable when mapped population and the underlying city population grow together, while a smaller city can have high density but modest programme burden. The null density result, positive count and kothikhana associations, and common-panel growth contrast therefore answer different questions rather than contradicting one another.

### Interpretation in relation to other evidence

The 66.3% common-city increase during deterioration is compatible with greater pressure on entry or return. Lahore evidence identifies financial need and survival as dominant reasons for entry and continuation. Economic shocks in Kenya and Malawi and historical recessions have likewise increased sexual-economic exchange or less formal sex-work supply. [25-27] These studies support a mechanism but do not establish why each additional person was mapped in Pakistan.

The Pakistani change was organisational as well as numerical: home-based work increased while street-based work declined. Zimbabwe provides the closest comparison, where economic collapse weakened some formal demand but dispersed exchange into private, informal, and commodity-based forms. Population estimates can also change with mapping reach and visibility, particularly for mobile or hidden work. [28] The observed Pakistani growth probably combines population dynamics with changes in organisation and ascertainment.

Recovery affected growth more clearly than the accumulated population. Longitudinal studies describe extended occupational duration, difficult exit, and re-entry after economic shocks. [29,30] Conversely, improved outside opportunities in Zambia and Cambodia reduced transactional sex or sex work. The Pakistani plateau is therefore consistent with lower net entry during recovery combined with barriers to exit, but entry and exit were not observed directly.

Demand can offset supply. During downturns, more women may need income while clients have less to spend; during recovery, entry pressure may ease while client demand returns. This helps explain unstable city rankings and why population, work setting, and density need not move in one direction.

### Implications, strengths, and limitations

HIV programmes should monitor absolute mapped population, common-city growth, work setting, and network organisation alongside density. Movement from streets to homes or intermediary-linked settings may reduce contact with testing, condoms, pre-exposure prophylaxis, violence support, and treatment referral. Outreach should therefore be flexible across peer, phone, home, and network channels. Economic recovery should not be expected to reverse an accumulated population without credible voluntary livelihood, housing, and safety options.

This study used the complete published Pakistani surveillance series, retained each source's analytical grain, separated primary from exploratory inference, and tested sensitivity to geography, influential cities, functional form, and multiplicity. However, mapping estimates are programme estimates rather than censuses. Coverage, field intensity, definitions, mobility, network reach, and visibility changed between rounds. Surveillance cities were selected for programme priorities, city outcomes were paired with district measures, only 2011 reported comparable density, and the 2014 sample contained four Punjab cities. The primary interval was wide, only one of 54 cross-sectional associations survived adjustment, and none of 32 macro-marker correlations survived exact inference. Ecological associations cannot identify individual entry, exit, earnings, prices, or client demand.

## Conclusion

Economic deterioration in Pakistan coincided with rapid growth and reorganisation of the mapped female sex worker population. Improved conditions coincided with sharp growth deceleration toward a plateau rather than immediate population decline. Density alone was not an informative economic marker; population growth, absolute programme burden, and organisation carried the clearer signal. Longitudinal surveillance should measure stable denominators, mapping intensity, entry and exit, earnings, client demand, mobility, and work setting so that the mechanisms underlying this relationship can be tested directly.
