# Literature synthesis for Paper 1

## Bottom line

The literature makes our Pakistan result more—not less—interpretable.

The best-supported explanation is a **two-sided market response**:

1. When households and women lose economic options, more women may enter, return to, or increase sex work because they need income.
2. At the same time, men may have less money to spend, so client demand and earnings can fall.
3. Work then often becomes less visible: it moves from formal venues or streets toward homes, phones, intermediaries, informal venues, mobility, or looser transactional arrangements.
4. When the economy improves, new economically forced entry may slow, but the already-existing population does not immediately disappear because exit is difficult and re-entry is common.

That pattern fits the complete Pakistani series: fast growth during deterioration, a move from street toward home/intermediary organisation, and much slower growth toward a plateau during recovery.

## We asked: does economic deterioration push more women toward sex work?

**What we found in Pakistan:** In identical-city panels, mapped counts grew rapidly from 2006–07 to 2011: 9.65% annualised in the four-city panel and 14.39% in the six-city panel. The strongest 2011 city relationships were with absolute market size and organisation, not density.

**What the literature says:** Yes, there is a credible mechanism and direct comparative evidence. In Lahore, 91% of surveyed women reported poverty or financial reasons for entry and 99% economic survival for continuation (PK04). In western Kenya, women increased risky, better-paid transactional sex after household health shocks (INT01). Drought doubled transactional sex among agricultural women in Malawi (INT03). A historical quasi-experiment found roughly 20% more sex-work establishments in recession-exposed British counties (INT04).

**Synthesis:** The direction of the Pakistani deterioration-period expansion is consistent with direct individual, population, and quasi-experimental evidence. Our ecological data still do not count entrants, so “more newcomers” remains the most plausible interpretation rather than a directly observed transition.

## We asked: why did street share fall while home and intermediary forms rose?

**What we found in Pakistan:** From 2006–07 to 2011, home share rose 18.32 percentage points, street share fell 24.16 points, and kothikhana share rose 6.99 points. Within 2011, worse reported conditions were positively associated with kothikhana/intermediary organisation and negatively associated with cellphone-only work.

**What the literature says:** The Zimbabwe reconfiguration study is the closest match (ZW01). During collapse, formal commercial demand weakened, but poverty-driven exchange became more diffuse, private, informal, and commodity based. The British recession study similarly found that three-quarters of the expansion occurred in informal rather than formal venues (INT04). Pakistan’s own 2006-to-2011 comparison documents rising cellphone and intermediary organisation (PK07).

**Synthesis:** A decline in visible street work is not evidence that the market shrank. It can mean that the market reorganised. Pakistan’s homeward shift is therefore a substantive outcome, not a nuisance detail.

## We asked: could a worsening economy reduce sex work instead?

**What we found in Pakistan:** The macro-marker results do not show one uniform inverse relationship across every marker. Some indicators align with supply expansion or homeward reorganisation, while density is weak and kothikhana share is not a clean longitudinal marker.

**What the literature says:** Yes—through demand. Zimbabwean evidence suggests that layoffs and the collapse of migrant labour reduced male client demand, client volumes, and STI rates; demand returned after recovery (ZW02). UK and Kenyan evidence also describes declining demand and income among existing sex workers during downturns or restrictions (INT05, INT13).

**Synthesis:** “Bad economy = more sex work” is incomplete. Bad conditions can push supply outward and demand inward at the same time. The observed result depends on which marker is measured: number of workers, clients, prices, earnings, venues, visibility, mobility, or risk. This explains why density alone was a poor sentinel marker in our data.

## We asked: what should better economic conditions do?

**What we found in Pakistan:** Comparable-city growth slowed sharply after 2011. Through 2014 it fell from 9.65% to 5.30% annualised in four cities; through 2016–17 it fell from 14.39% to 0.87% in six cities. Counts did not reverse.

**What the literature says:** Better conditions can reduce the pressure for new entry, but they may also restore client purchasing power. A Zambian copper boom substantially reduced transactional sex and multiple partnerships (INT17). A Cambodian field experiment found that improving non-sex-work earning options increased outside earnings by 20% and reduced sex work by 13%, whereas an unconditional cash transfer did not reduce sex work (INT18). Zimbabwe shows the other side: client demand returned after 2009 recovery (ZW02). Entry/exit studies show that people do not leave automatically when conditions improve: economic security, alternative employment, relationships, housing, violence, and stigma all shape exit (INT06–INT10, INT19).

**Synthesis:** A plateau is a meaningful response. It is consistent with fewer net newcomers after recovery while established workers remain, and with countervailing recovery of demand. A falling total was never the only defensible expectation.

## We asked: is the plateau interpretation defensible?

**What we found in Pakistan:** The survey series measures population stock at repeated points, not entries, exits, or individual careers.

**What the literature says:** Long careers and repeated exit/re-entry are common. In the London cohort, 59% remained in the industry after a mean 13.6 years (INT07). In Thailand, almost every interviewed woman had quit at least once and most cycled in and out, with economics the strongest factor (INT06). Kigali accounts link financial crises to return (INT08); Baltimore follow-up found that 40% stopped at least once and some restarted, while homelessness and violence reduced interruptions (INT10). A 2025 Malawi longitudinal narrative study found that 20 of 30 women had temporarily exited at least once and most returned when circumstances worsened (INT19).

**Synthesis:** It is defensible to say the plateau is **consistent with** fewer net newcomers plus difficult exit. It is not defensible to say the surveys prove that mechanism, because they do not follow individuals.

## We asked: why not use density as the single marker?

**What we found in Pakistan:** The prespecified 2011 density correlation was weak (Spearman rho −0.138, p=.620), while deterioration aligned more clearly with absolute market size and organisation. Chronic deprivation and acute deterioration also pointed in different directions for total counts and density.

**What the literature says:** Pakistani mapping papers show strong city heterogeneity and large concentration in Karachi and Lahore (PK02, PK06, PK10). Zimbabwe population-size work shows that estimates vary by method and site (ZW03). Mobility can redistribute who is visible in a location (ZW05).

**Synthesis:** Density answers one question—mapped workers relative to a denominator. It does not capture total market scale, entry rate, client demand, work form, or movement. The literature supports our marker set: comparable-city count growth, slowdown/plateau, home-versus-street composition, intermediary organisation, and density only where valid denominators exist.

## We asked: does the wider literature prove Pakistan’s economic relationship?

No. It makes the interpretation plausible and shows that the pattern has precedents. The Pakistan finding itself comes from Pakistan’s complete available surveillance series paired with contemporaneous socioeconomic measures. International studies supply mechanisms and comparators; they do not replace local identification.

## What is strongest enough to say in a paper

> Across the complete available Pakistani surveillance series, deterioration coincided with rapid growth in mapped FSW populations and a shift away from street-based work toward home/intermediary organisation, while subsequent recovery coincided with marked deceleration toward a plateau. This pattern is consistent with a two-sided economic response described elsewhere: hardship can increase the supply of women seeking income from sex work while reducing client demand and shifting activity into less visible forms. The persistence of the mapped stock during recovery is also consistent with documented barriers to permanent exit and recurrent re-entry. Because the Pakistan surveys do not observe individual transitions and mapping sensitivity can change, these mechanisms remain interpretations rather than directly measured causal pathways.

## What not to say

- Do not say poverty mechanically causes sex work.
- Do not say every downturn raises every FSW marker.
- Do not treat transactional sex as identical to mapped professional sex work.
- Do not claim that the plateau proves fewer entrants; say it is consistent with fewer net newcomers and exit barriers.
- Do not use the Zimbabwe demand contraction as a contradiction of our finding; it is the missing second side of the market.
- Do not use HIV or STI changes as direct substitutes for population-size change.
- Do not imply mapping estimates are a complete census or that cross-round discoverability is constant.
