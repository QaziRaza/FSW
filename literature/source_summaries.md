# Source summaries

These are original evidence summaries, not copied abstracts. “Use in Paper 1” states the precise job each source can do in a future Discussion. Full abstracts or reports are available at the linked pages.

## Pakistan: direct and foundational evidence

### PK01 — NACP Round II, 2006–07

- **Source:** National AIDS Control Programme. *HIV Second Generation Surveillance in Pakistan: National Report Round II* (2007). [Report](https://www.nacp.gov.pk/repository/howwework/pdf/SGS%20Round%202%20Report%202006-07.pdf)
- **What it did:** Mapped and surveyed key populations, including FSWs, across Pakistani cities.
- **What it found:** It provides city FSW counts and work typologies for the earliest round used here. The acquired report does not provide a comparable city density denominator.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Primary evidence for the 2006–07 baseline and the deterioration-to-2011 change; cite the table and page actually used by the dataset.
- **Main caution:** Later rounds changed city coverage and mapping visibility, so only common-city and comparable-marker changes should be interpreted.

### PK02 — Blanchard, Khan, and Bokhari, 2008

- **Source:** “Variations in the population size, distribution and client volume among female sex workers in seven cities of Pakistan.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18799488/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/sti.2008.033167)
- **What it did:** Compared mapped FSW populations, work settings, and client volumes across seven cities.
- **What it found:** It mapped 34,480 FSWs; roughly 40% were street based and 57.5% home based, with strong city and client-volume heterogeneity.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports the decision to examine counts, density, typology, and market structure separately rather than treat “FSW prevalence” as a single uniform measure.
- **Main caution:** Cross-sectional and not an economic-effects study.

### PK03 — Pakistan situation assessment, 2009

- **Source:** *Situation Assessment of Sex Work and HIV Prevention in Pakistan*. [Report](https://new.aidsdatahub.org/sites/default/files/resource/situation-assessment-sex-work-hiv-prevention-pakistan-2009.pdf)
- **What it did:** Synthesised the legal, social, organisational, and programme environment surrounding sex work and HIV prevention.
- **What it found:** Pakistani sex work operates under high stigma and limited visibility, with substantial organisational diversity and prevention-access constraints.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Background for why mapped visibility, venue type, and programme reach can change independently of the true underlying population.
- **Main caution:** Contextual grey literature, not a causal test of economic conditions.

### PK04 — Khan et al., 2011

- **Source:** “HIV, STI prevalence and risk behaviours among women selling sex in Lahore, Pakistan.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569319/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3114735/)
- **What it did:** Respondent-driven survey of 730 women selling sex in three Lahore areas.
- **What it found:** Ninety-one percent cited poverty or financial reasons for starting, 99% cited economic survival for continuing, and median time selling sex was seven years.
- **Use in Paper 1:** The clearest direct Pakistani evidence connecting economic need to both entry and continuation; it also makes persistence plausible.
- **Main caution:** Retrospective self-report from one city; it does not estimate how macroeconomic change alters population size.

### PK05 — NACP Round IV, 2011

- **Source:** National AIDS Control Programme. *HIV Second Generation Surveillance in Pakistan: National Report Round IV*. [Report](https://www.anf.gov.pk/library/report-round4-2011.pdf)
- **What it did:** National mapping followed by behavioural and biological surveillance in 20 cities.
- **What it found:** It supplies the most complete common-round FSW population, typology, and behavioural evidence in the available series.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Primary source for the 2011 city outcomes against which district economic conditions are mapped.
- **Main caution:** Population-size values are programme mapping estimates, not a census; discoverability can change.

### PK06 — Emmanuel et al., 2013, organisation and structure

- **Source:** “The organisation, operational dynamics and structure of female sex work in Pakistan.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23771032/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3756447/)
- **What it did:** Analysed the 2011 mapping for 15 reported city units.
- **What it found:** It estimated 89,178 FSWs, an average 7.2 per 1,000 adult men, with 55% in Karachi and Lahore and marked variation in home, street, brothel, kothikhana, and intermediary organisation.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct source for the primary outcomes and strong justification for the multi-marker interpretation.
- **Main caution:** Descriptive mapping; density is denominator-sensitive and says something different from absolute market size.

### PK07 — Mishra et al., 2013

- **Source:** “Sexual behaviour, structural vulnerabilities and HIV prevalence among female sex workers in Pakistan.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23413402/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3756444/)
- **What it did:** Compared 2006 and 2011 IBBS data across nine common Pakistani cities.
- **What it found:** Mapping estimates rose from 49,037 to 73,675. Cellphone solicitation, mobile-operator and kothikhana-linked organisation, reported clients, and several vulnerability markers increased.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Published corroboration that the deterioration period involved expansion and a change in how the market was organised.
- **Main caution:** The authors explicitly note that improved mapping and surveillance may explain part of the increase.

### PK08 — Emmanuel et al., 2013, surveillance system

- **Source:** “Second-generation surveillance for HIV/AIDS in Pakistan: results from the 4th round of Integrated Behavior and Biological Survey 2011–2012.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23912818/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3841725/)
- **What it did:** Described Pakistan’s second-generation surveillance system and reported Round IV results.
- **What it found:** The mapping estimated 89,178 FSWs across the covered cities, followed by IBBS sampling and biological testing.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Establishes how the stock estimate was produced and why mapping and survey samples are distinct.
- **Main caution:** The surveillance was designed for HIV programming, not specifically for economic causal inference.

### PK09 — Emmanuel et al., 2013, resource allocation

- **Source:** “The size and distribution of key populations at greater risk of HIV in Pakistan: implications for resource allocation...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23843454/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3756450/)
- **What it did:** Used 2011 mapped population sizes and programme costs to model prevention allocation.
- **What it found:** Concentrating coverage in ten major cities could reach large shares of key populations at an estimated annual operating cost well below then-current spending.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Shows that absolute mapped population size has programme meaning that per-capita density does not replace.
- **Main caution:** Cost and prioritisation model, not an economic determinant analysis.

### PK10 — Reza et al., 2013

- **Source:** “Patterns and trends in Pakistan’s heterogeneous HIV epidemic.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23633670/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3756441/)
- **What it did:** Combined 2011 densities, network bridging data, and epidemic models across selected cities.
- **What it found:** Population density and links between FSWs, other sex-worker groups, and people who inject drugs varied sharply by city.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports city-specific analysis and demonstrates that identical national economic conditions need not produce identical local outcomes.
- **Main caution:** Models HIV trajectories, not changes in the number entering or leaving sex work.

### PK11 — Punjab IBBS, 2014

- **Source:** Punjab AIDS Control Program. *Integrated Biological and Behavioral Surveillance in Punjab 2014*. [Report](https://phkh.nhsrc.pk/sites/default/files/2021-01/IBBS%20Punjab%202014.pdf)
- **What it did:** Mapped and surveyed key populations in Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan, and Sargodha.
- **What it found:** Supplies city FSW counts and typology shares during the initial recovery period.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct source for the four-city 2011-to-2014 change and typology reversal comparison.
- **Main caution:** Regional rather than national; no comparable adult-male density was published.

### PK12 — NACP mapping, 2015–16

- **Source:** National AIDS Control Programme. *Mapping of Key Populations in Pakistan 2015*. [Landing page](https://medbox.org/document/mapping-of-key-populations-in-pakistan-2015)
- **What it did:** Updated key-population mapping for later surveillance.
- **What it found:** Confirms a later mapping exercise, but the acquired executive-summary excerpt does not contain the complete city table required for the present comparisons.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Source provenance and evidence of a measurement gap.
- **Main caution:** Do not infer missing city values or typology shares from the excerpt.

### PK13 — NACP Round V, 2016–17

- **Source:** National AIDS Control Programme. *Integrated Biological and Behavioral Surveillance in Pakistan, Round 5*. [Report](https://www.nacp.gov.pk/repository/howwework/pdf/IBBS%20Report%20Round%205.pdf)
- **What it did:** Later national mapping and IBBS.
- **What it found:** Provides printed city-average FSW counts, but those values sum to 71,315 while the printed grand total is 64,829; no comparable city density or typology table was found.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports the six-city 2011-to-2016/17 stock-growth comparison, with the internal inconsistency disclosed.
- **Main caution:** Use common-city values only and do not treat the national total as internally validated.

### PK14 — Melesse et al., 2018

- **Source:** “Heterogeneity in geographical trends of HIV epidemics among key populations in Pakistan.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29770215/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5944903/)
- **What it did:** Fit epidemic models to four mapping/surveillance rounds across up to 20 cities.
- **What it found:** Modelled HIV incidence and prevalence trends differed markedly by key population and city.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Reinforces the decision not to force all cities and rounds into one homogeneous national model.
- **Main caution:** Modelled HIV incidence is not a measure of FSW population entry or size.

### PK15 — Anwar and Safdar, 2014

- **Source:** “Socio-Economic and Psychological Causes of Female Prostitution in Punjab (A Case Study of District Sahiwal).” [Article](https://www.richtmann.org/journal/index.php/jesr/article/view/2738)
- **What it did:** Interviewed ten purposively selected respondents in Sahiwal.
- **What it found:** Described socioeconomic and psychological entry circumstances, violence, and social-adjustment problems.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Local qualitative context only.
- **Main caution:** Very small, non-representative sample and limited reporting; it cannot establish prevalence or trends.

### PK16 — Iram et al., 2019

- **Source:** “Socioeconomic acceptability of prostitutes in Pakistan: A diversion from prostitution.” [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/99846b4e73de406eb9acf6d0080caf3c) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/23311886.2019.1646110)
- **What it did:** Examined reported reasons for entry and barriers to social and economic reintegration.
- **What it found:** Highlights poverty, low pay, illiteracy, social exclusion, and gender inequality as recurring conditions.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supplementary Pakistani livelihood context.
- **Main caution:** Method reporting and normative framing make it lower-weight evidence than the national surveillance and Lahore RDS study.

## Zimbabwe: closest comparison

### ZW01 — Elmes et al., 2017

- **Source:** “A reconfiguration of the sex trade...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28225822/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321466/)
- **What it did:** Reconstructed how economic collapse and social change altered sex work and transactional sex in eastern Zimbabwe.
- **What it found:** Formal venue-based demand weakened, but poverty-driven exchange increased and activity became more informal, dispersed, private, and often commodity based.
- **Use in Paper 1:** The closest substantive match to the Pakistani combination of deterioration, population expansion, and movement away from visible street work toward home/intermediary organisation.
- **Main caution:** Qualitative historical case study; it identifies mechanisms but does not estimate a Pakistan-sized effect.

### ZW02 — Steen et al., 2019

- **Source:** “Economy, migrant labour and sex work: interplay of HIV epidemic drivers in Zimbabwe over three decades.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30475261/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6415983/)
- **What it did:** Integrated three decades of economic, labour, behavioural, STI, and HIV evidence.
- **What it found:** Layoffs and collapsing migrant labour reduced male demand, client volumes, and STI rates; client demand and sex-work activity strengthened again after recovery.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Essential qualification: economic decline can raise women’s need to sell sex while reducing men’s ability to buy it. The net mapped result depends on both sides.
- **Main caution:** Narrative synthesis across changing data sources; several relationships are historical rather than individually linked.

### ZW03 — Fearon et al., 2020

- **Source:** “Estimating the Population Size of Female Sex Workers in Zimbabwe...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32379082/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7417013/)
- **What it did:** Compared census, capture–recapture, service-multiplier, and unique-object estimates in 20 sites and extrapolated nationally.
- **What it found:** Estimated 40,491 FSWs nationally, with wide plausibility bounds and large method/site differences.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Demonstrates that hidden-population counts carry real method uncertainty; movements in estimates should be read with source consistency and triangulation.
- **Main caution:** Later Zimbabwe setting and different estimation methods.

### ZW04 — Busza et al., 2014

- **Source:** “Triple jeopardy: adolescent experiences of sex work and migration in Zimbabwe.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24791742/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2014.04.002)
- **What it did:** Interviewed ten women who had experienced adolescent sex work and migration.
- **What it found:** Family instability, political upheaval, inability to pay school fees, and a search for independence contributed to mobility and entry; sex work then encouraged further income-seeking migration.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports pathways from macro disruption to individual entry and movement.
- **Main caution:** Small, difficult-to-recruit, retrospective sample.

### ZW05 — Davey et al., 2019

- **Source:** “Mobility and sex work: why, where, when?” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30500610/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2018.11.027)
- **What it did:** Compared respondent-driven samples of 2,591 FSWs in 2013 and 2,839 in 2016 across 14 sites.
- **What it found:** Work travel rose from 27% to 59%; seeking clients with more money motivated 57% of journeys.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Shows that an economic response may appear as spatial redistribution and changing detectability rather than a simple local count change.
- **Main caution:** Repeated cross-sections, not individual longitudinal tracking.

## Comparable mechanisms and interpretation

### INT01 — Robinson and Yeh, 2011

- **Source:** “Transactional Sex as a Response to Risk in Western Kenya.” [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.3.1.35)
- **What it did:** Analysed a panel of 192 detailed self-reported diaries.
- **What it found:** Women increased the amount of risky, better-paid transactional sex after unexpected household health shocks, especially illness of another household member.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct micro-level evidence that economic need can change sex supply quickly when other consumption-smoothing options fail.
- **Main caution:** Transactional sex among an existing group is not the same outcome as mapped FSW population size.

### INT02 — Burke, Gong, and Jones, 2015

- **Source:** “Income Shocks and HIV in Africa.” [Article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ecoj.12149)
- **What it did:** Linked rainfall-based income shocks to HIV biomarkers for more than 200,000 people in 21 surveys from 19 African countries.
- **What it found:** In high-prevalence rural areas, each recent drought shock was associated with an estimated 11% rise in HIV risk; transactional sex was a supported mechanism.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Strong evidence that exogenous livelihood shocks can alter sexual-economic behaviour at population scale.
- **Main caution:** HIV is downstream and the transactional-sex mechanism is not itself a measured FSW count.

### INT03 — Treibich et al., 2022

- **Source:** “From a drought to HIV...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36164494/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9508466/)
- **What it did:** Linked Malawi’s 2015–16 DHS to a severe six-month drought.
- **What it found:** Among women working in agriculture, drought exposure doubled transactional sex and increased reported STI by 48%; effects were strongest among less-educated women.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct economic-shock evidence for increased sexual-economic coping.
- **Main caution:** Agricultural shock and transactional-sex outcome differ from urban Pakistan mapping.

### INT04 — Goehring, 2025

- **Source:** “Fallen women: Recessions and the supply of sex work.” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2025.105405)
- **What it did:** Used a difference-in-differences design around the 1861–65 British cotton famine, which disproportionately affected women’s employment.
- **What it found:** Exposed counties had about 20% more sex-work establishments; three-quarters of the increase was in informal establishments, even while a leisure-demand proxy fell.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Strong direct evidence that recession can expand supply and shift activity toward informal settings despite weaker demand—the same two-sided logic needed here.
- **Main caution:** Historical, legal, and labour-market context is very different from Pakistan.

### INT05 — Jarvis-King, 2024

- **Source:** “Trajectories of Vulnerability and Resistance Among Independent Indoor Sex Workers During Economic Decline.” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13607804231162757)
- **What it did:** Qualitatively followed independent indoor sex workers after the 2007–08 financial crisis.
- **What it found:** A weaker mainstream labour market made sex work a coping choice, while new competition and falling client demand reduced earnings and wellbeing.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Clean statement of the simultaneous supply-push and demand-contraction mechanism.
- **Main caution:** UK indoor market and qualitative sample.

### INT06 — Manopaiboon et al., 2003

- **Source:** “Leaving sex work: barriers, facilitating factors and consequences...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12655832/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/012021000039743)
- **What it did:** Interviewed 42 current and former FSWs from a northern Thailand cohort.
- **What it found:** All but one had left at least once, most experienced quit–re-entry–quit cycles, and economic conditions had the strongest influence on leaving.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct support for interpreting a plateau as fewer net additions rather than mass permanent exit.
- **Main caution:** Qualitative subsample and a different national setting.

### INT07 — Ward and Day, 2006

- **Source:** “What happens to women who sell sex? Report of a unique occupational cohort.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16790559/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2563855/)
- **What it did:** Traced outcomes in 130 women from a London cohort over 15 years.
- **What it found:** Fifty-nine percent were still in the sex industry and mean duration among those continuing was 13.6 years, although occupational movement was common.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Demonstrates that the population stock can remain high long after entry conditions improve.
- **Main caution:** Only 37% of the original cohort had outcome data and the setting differs.

### INT08 — Ingabire et al., 2012

- **Source:** “Joining and leaving sex work: experiences of women in Kigali, Rwanda.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22937751/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13691058.2012.713120)
- **What it did:** Conducted focus groups and interviews with 70 FSWs.
- **What it found:** Poverty and disruptive events shaped entry; finance was the dominant reason for staying, and some who left returned after another financial crisis.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports both entry under hardship and non-permanent exit.
- **Main caution:** Qualitative accounts cannot estimate population-level transition rates.

### INT09 — Learmonth, Hakala, and Keller, 2015

- **Source:** “I can’t carry on like this: barriers to exiting the street-based sex trade in South Africa.” [Full article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21642850.2015.1095098)
- **What it did:** Analysed two focus groups involving 21 women.
- **What it found:** Poverty, weak alternative employment, stigma, trauma, and violence constrained exit; one reported returning after loss of a conventional job.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Supports the idea that recovery alone may not remove established workers from the stock.
- **Main caution:** Small qualitative sample of street-based workers.

### INT10 — White et al., 2020

- **Source:** “Short-term interruptions to sex work...” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32726687/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7738417/)
- **What it did:** Followed 205 street-based FSWs quarterly for 12 months in Baltimore.
- **What it found:** Forty percent stopped at least once; homelessness and intimate-partner violence made interruption less likely, and 10% stopped then restarted.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Quantitative evidence that exit is episodic and that structural vulnerability maintains participation.
- **Main caution:** Street-based US sample with substance-use pathways that may not generalise.

### INT11 — McClarty et al., 2014

- **Source:** “Circumstances, experiences and processes surrounding women’s entry into sex work in India.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24236895/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/13691058.2013.845692)
- **What it did:** Reviewed Indian evidence on women’s early sex-work careers.
- **What it found:** Financial insecurity, family discord, violence, coercion, tradition, and desire for independence all contributed; family and community shaped entry.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Comparable South Asian evidence that an economic signal is real but embedded in social mechanisms.
- **Main caution:** Narrative review and heterogeneous underlying studies.

### INT12 — Saggurti et al., 2011

- **Source:** “Motivations for entry into sex work and HIV risk among mobile female sex workers in India.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21729360/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0021932011000277)
- **What it did:** Surveyed 5,498 mobile FSWs across 22 districts in four states.
- **What it found:** Poor economic conditions were a common entry reason and were associated with higher current risk than entry by choice or family tradition.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Large regional comparator supporting heterogeneous entry pathways with a strong economic component.
- **Main caution:** Cross-sectional retrospective motivation and a mobile-only population.

### INT13 — Kimani et al., 2020

- **Source:** “The effects of COVID-19 on the health and socio-economic security of sex workers in Nairobi, Kenya.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32459578/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17441692.2020.1770831)
- **What it did:** Rapidly synthesised programme and community evidence during pandemic restrictions.
- **What it found:** Curfews and income loss severely damaged food, housing, health, and economic security and altered work-related risks.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Reminder that an economic shock can harm existing workers and reduce market activity even when more women need income.
- **Main caution:** Commentary-style rapid evidence rather than a population-size study.

### INT14 — Shannon et al., 2015

- **Source:** “Global epidemiology of HIV among female sex workers: influence of structural determinants.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25059947/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297548/)
- **What it did:** Reviewed the global evidence and modelled structural interventions.
- **What it found:** Macro structures, laws, policing, violence, migration, community organisation, and work environments jointly shape risk and access.
- **Use in Paper 1:** The conceptual guardrail against treating GDP, inflation, or poverty as a complete explanation by itself.
- **Main caution:** Focused primarily on HIV outcomes, not population entry or counts.

### INT15 — Wamoyi et al., 2016

- **Source:** “Transactional sex and risk for HIV infection in sub-Saharan Africa.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27809960/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5095351/)
- **What it did:** Systematically reviewed 19 papers from 16 studies and meta-analysed comparable subsets.
- **What it found:** Transactional sex was associated with HIV among women; the pooled unadjusted odds ratio was 1.54 in the comparable female subset, but few studies were longitudinal.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Helps distinguish the broader transactional-sex mechanism from formally mapped sex work.
- **Main caution:** It does not establish that economic conditions increase the number of FSWs.

### INT16 — Mihretie et al., 2023

- **Source:** “Transactional sex among women in Sub-Saharan Africa: A systematic review and meta-analysis.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37289839/) | [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10249834/)
- **What it did:** Synthesised 48 studies published from 2000 to 2022.
- **What it found:** Pooled prevalence was 12.55%, with substantial heterogeneity; violence, orphanhood, early sexual debut, and substance use were associated factors.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Shows that financial pressure operates inside a broader vulnerability system and that prevalence varies substantially by population and method.
- **Main caution:** Transactional sex is defined more broadly than sex work and the meta-analysis was highly heterogeneous.

### INT17 — Wilson, 2012

- **Source:** “Economic booms and risky sexual behavior: evidence from Zambian copper mining cities.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22940637/) | [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhealeco.2012.07.007)
- **What it did:** Used the early-2000s copper boom to compare changes in risky sexual behaviour in mining cities with other Zambian locations.
- **What it found:** The boom substantially reduced transactional sex and multiple partnerships, with part of the change concentrated among young adults.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Direct evidence for our deliberately tested opposite direction: better economic conditions can reduce transactional sexual exchange.
- **Main caution:** Transactional sex and partnerships are not mapped FSW population stock, and boom-related migration may contribute to the result.

### INT18 — Mahadeshwar and Zhou, 2024 working paper

- **Source:** “Outside Options and the Supply of Sex Work.” [Working paper](https://www.isid.ac.in/~acegd/acegd2024/papers/RuchiMahadeshwar.pdf)
- **What it did:** Randomised incentives that improved outside-work opportunities and a separate unconditional cash transfer among sex workers in Cambodia.
- **What it found:** The outside-work intervention raised outside earnings by 20% and reduced sex work by 13%; unconditional cash alone did not reduce sex work.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Strong evidence that the quality and return to alternative employment—not simply more money—can reduce sex-work labour supply.
- **Main caution:** Working paper, not yet treated as a final peer-reviewed publication; verify the latest version before manuscript submission.

### INT19 — Lora et al., 2025

- **Source:** “Exploring the Interplay of Social and Physical Factors in Risk Dynamics and Transitions Across the Life-Course of Female Sex Workers in Blantyre, Malawi.” [Article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10461-025-04790-z)
- **What it did:** Interviewed 30 FSWs three times over 12 months and reconstructed entry, slowing, breaks, and re-entry across their life courses.
- **What it found:** Twenty participants had temporarily exited at least once; most returned when circumstances worsened. Financial stability supported slowing or breaks, while business failure, partner loss, violence, and marginalisation drove re-entry.
- **Use in Paper 1:** Recent longitudinal support for a fluid population stock in which improved conditions can slow participation without guaranteeing permanent exit.
- **Main caution:** Current FSWs were recruited, so permanent exiters are systematically underrepresented; retrospective histories remain subject to recall bias.
