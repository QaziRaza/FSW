# Discussion

## Principal findings

The complete published Pakistani survey series contains a coherent economic signal. In the 2011 cross-sectional analysis, cities reporting greater recent economic deterioration did not have higher mapped female sex worker (FSW) density: the association was weakly inverse (Spearman rho = -0.138; bootstrap 95% CI -0.597 to 0.372; permutation p = 0.620). Density was also weakly and inconsistently associated with poverty, female illiteracy, and sanitation deprivation. The more informative signals appeared in the size, growth, and organisation of the mapped population. Recent deterioration was positively associated with absolute mapped FSW numbers (rho = 0.657) and with kothikhana-based organisation (rho = 0.618), and inversely associated with cellphone-based work (rho = -0.515). The all-area deterioration measure had an even stronger association with kothikhana share (rho = 0.771; false-discovery-rate-adjusted q = 0.041), the only association in the full set of cross-sectional comparisons to retain a 5% multiplicity-adjusted threshold.

The longitudinal comparisons provide the clearest substantive result. Across ten cities observed in both 2006-07 and 2011, the combined mapped FSW population increased by 66.3% during a period in which GDP growth weakened, inflation rose, and reported household deterioration increased. In the strict continuous panels, annualised mapped-population growth subsequently fell from 9.65% during deterioration to 5.30% during recovery in the four cities observed through 2014, and from 14.39% to 0.87% in the six cities observed through 2016-17. The later rate in the six-city panel was only 6.1% of the earlier rate. Thus, the observed pattern was rapid expansion during worsening conditions followed by strong deceleration toward a plateau as conditions improved. It was not expansion followed by the immediate disappearance of the accumulated population.

These results support a more complete answer to the study question. Economic conditions are related to the dynamics of Pakistan's mapped FSW population, but the relationship is expressed more clearly through population growth and market organisation than through a single density measure. This interpretation is consistent with Pakistan's own surveillance literature, which documents substantial growth, marked between-city heterogeneity, and changes in how sex work was organised across survey rounds [@NACP2007RoundII; @NACP2011RoundIV; @Emmanuel2013Organisation; @Mishra2013Vulnerabilities; @NACP2017RoundV].

## Why density did not capture the main relationship

The weak primary density result should not be interpreted as evidence that economic conditions are unrelated to sex work. A density is a ratio: it can remain stable when both the mapped FSW population and the adult-male denominator grow, and it can be high in a smaller city even when the absolute market is modest. In the 2011 data, recent deterioration was associated with larger mapped totals and larger implied city scale, but not with higher FSWs per 1,000 adult men. Chronic deprivation showed almost the opposite configuration: it was associated with smaller totals and smaller city scale, but somewhat higher density. Recent economic worsening and long-standing deprivation therefore describe different kinds of urban economy and should not be treated as interchangeable measures of “poverty.”

This distinction accords with earlier Pakistani studies. Mapping has shown large variations between cities in population size, concentration, work setting, and client volume [@Blanchard2008Variation; @Emmanuel2013Organisation; @Reza2013Patterns]. Population-size estimates for hidden and mobile populations are also sensitive to geographic coverage, visibility, and method, as demonstrated directly in Zimbabwe [@Fearon2020ZimbabwePSE]. Absolute count, density, and organisational form consequently answer different questions. Density describes relative concentration; the count describes the number of people and the potential programme burden; typology describes how the market is operating. Our results suggest that the latter two dimensions carried more of the economic signal in Pakistan.

The failure of linear, quadratic, and cubic density models to outperform a constant-only model reinforces this interpretation. There was no evidence of a U-shaped or threshold density relationship. The appropriate conclusion is not that the economy had no relationship with the FSW population, but that density alone did not adequately represent that relationship.

## Deterioration, expansion, and market reorganisation

The rapid common-city increase between 2006-07 and 2011 is compatible with a supply response to economic stress. In the Lahore study, financial need and economic survival were overwhelmingly reported as reasons for entry and continuation in sex work, and the median duration in sex work was seven years [@Khan2011Lahore]. Evidence from other settings gives the same mechanism greater plausibility. Short-term health and income shocks increased higher-paid, riskier transactional sex in western Kenya [@RobinsonYeh2011Risk]; drought increased transactional sex in Malawi [@Treibich2022Drought]; and historical recession evidence indicates that economic contractions can expand the supply of sex work, particularly in less formal settings [@Goehring2025FallenWomen]. These studies do not prove that each additional person mapped in Pakistan entered because of worsening economic conditions, but they establish that economic shocks can change the supply of sexual-economic exchange in the direction observed here.

The Pakistani change was not only numerical. From 2006-07 to 2011, median home-based share increased by 18.3 percentage points, street-based share fell by 24.2 points, and kothikhana share rose by 7.0 points among common cities. This is important because worsening conditions need not produce a visibly larger street market. They can instead alter where transactions are arranged, how workers connect with clients, and the role of intermediaries. Pakistan's repeated-surveillance analysis likewise reported growth alongside changes in cellphone and intermediary-mediated arrangements [@Mishra2013Vulnerabilities].

The closest comparative result comes from eastern Zimbabwe. During severe economic collapse, formal commercial demand weakened, but poverty-driven sexual exchange did not simply vanish. It became more dispersed, informal, private, and sometimes commodity based [@Elmes2017Reconfiguration]. That finding closely matches the combination observed in Pakistan: rapid mapped-population growth and movement away from street-based work, without a clean positive city-density relationship. It suggests that economic deterioration can reconfigure the market at the same time that it enlarges or redistributes the population exposed to it.

The 2011 association between deterioration and kothikhana organisation is consistent with this structural account. Intermediaries may become more important when workers need access to clients, protection, premises, or networks under changing economic conditions. However, kothikhana share continued to rise in the four-city comparison through 2014 even as growth improved and inflation fell. It is therefore useful as a cross-sectional organisational marker in 2011, but not as a universal longitudinal indicator of economic decline. Similarly, the inverse 2011 association with cellphone-only work should not be translated into a simple claim that phone-based work represents prosperity. Technology, mobility, intermediation, and work setting are related but distinct dimensions of organisation [@Davey2019Mobility; @Shannon2015Structural].

## What improved economic conditions appear to change

The recovery comparisons answer the inverse question that a single 2011 analysis could not: what happened when economic conditions improved? The mapped population did not immediately contract, but its growth rate collapsed. This is the pattern expected when the number of new entrants or returners falls while many people already in the profession remain. In stock-flow terms, economic deterioration can increase the inflow into sex work and enlarge the stock. Later improvement can reduce net inflow without rapidly reducing the existing stock, especially when exit is difficult.

The literature strongly supports the persistence component of this interpretation. Longitudinal and qualitative studies in Thailand, Rwanda, South Africa, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Malawi describe repeated attempts to leave, movement in and out of sex work, return after income or employment crises, and long occupational durations [@Manopaiboon2003Leaving; @WardDay2006Cohort; @Ingabire2012Kigali; @Learmonth2015Barriers; @White2020Interruptions; @Lora2025LifeCourse]. Economic need, limited alternative work, housing insecurity, violence, and sudden income loss can all prevent a desired exit or precipitate re-entry. Pakistan's Lahore evidence—where economic survival was a dominant reason for continuation and the median duration was already seven years—is consistent with such persistence [@Khan2011Lahore].

Evidence on improved outside options supports the inflow side of the same account. Economic expansion in Zambian copper-mining cities was associated with lower transactional sex and fewer multiple partnerships [@Wilson2012Booms]. A field experiment in Cambodia found that stronger incentives for outside work increased non-sex-work earnings and reduced sex work, whereas an unconditional cash transfer alone did not produce the same reduction [@MahadeshwarZhou2024Outside]. The latter remains working-paper evidence and concerns individual behaviour rather than mapped population stocks, but it sharpens the mechanism: better conditions matter most when they create a workable alternative source of livelihood, not merely when an aggregate indicator improves.

Taken together, our data and this literature make “fewer net newcomers plus difficult exit” the most coherent interpretation of the Pakistani plateau. It remains an interpretation rather than a directly measured flow because the Pakistani surveys enumerate population stocks and do not identify individual entries, exits, or returns. Nevertheless, it explains both parts of the observed pattern—rapid growth during deterioration and persistence with sharply slower growth during recovery—better than the expectation that a recovery should cause an immediate fall in the total.

## Supply and demand can move in opposite directions

Economic change affects both potential workers and potential clients. A downturn can increase the number of women needing income while simultaneously reducing what clients can spend. A recovery can ease entry pressure while restoring client demand. These opposing forces help explain why city-level changes in household distress were not consistently ranked with changes in mapped counts, even though the aggregate continuous panels showed a strong expansion-to-plateau sequence.

Zimbabwe provides direct evidence for the demand side. Layoffs and the collapse of migrant-labour income reduced male demand, client volume, and related sexually transmitted infection indicators; activity subsequently returned as the economy recovered after 2009 [@Steen2019Economy]. Recent research with independent indoor workers also describes the simultaneous effects of increased financial pressure, greater competition, and weakened client demand during economic decline [@JarvisKing2024Trajectories]. These findings clarify why the sign of an aggregate association need not be uniform across every city or market segment. The observed population at any round is the net result of entry, exit, re-entry, mobility, demand, earnings, and survey visibility.

This framework also resolves an apparent contradiction in our findings. Worse economic conditions can increase supply pressure and alter work setting without producing higher density in every city. Better conditions can reduce economically compelled entry while also improving client demand, leaving counts stable or even increasing temporarily. The key Pakistani result is therefore not a universal rule that “bad economy means more FSWs” at every place and time. It is that the deterioration period was associated with unusually rapid mapped-population growth and substantial organisational change, whereas the recovery period was associated with a pronounced slowdown in net growth.

## Acute deterioration and chronic deprivation are not the same exposure

Recent deterioration was inversely correlated with the chronic-deprivation index in 2011 (rho = -0.568). The cities reporting the greatest worsening tended to be larger and less chronically deprived, whereas persistently deprived cities were often smaller. This helps explain why acute deterioration related positively to absolute FSW totals but weakly inversely to density, while chronic deprivation related negatively to totals but somewhat positively to density.

The distinction has a plausible substantive basis. An acute shock measures loss relative to a city's prior position and may affect large labour and consumer markets even when their long-term deprivation level is not the highest. Chronic deprivation measures entrenched scarcity, weak services, and limited opportunity. These conditions may sustain a relatively high concentration of sex work in a smaller population without creating the largest absolute market. Structural reviews likewise show that sex work and HIV vulnerability are shaped by interacting labour markets, mobility, violence, policing, stigma, and access to services rather than by one poverty measure alone [@Shannon2015Structural].

This difference also cautions against treating studies of transactional sex in the general population as direct estimates of formal or mapped FSW populations. Transactional sex is broader and may include occasional or informal exchanges that surveillance mapping does not classify as sex work [@Wamoyi2016Transactional; @Mihretie2023Prevalence]. Such studies are useful for identifying mechanisms of economic pressure, but they cannot supply the denominator or population trajectory measured in the Pakistani mapping rounds.

## Public-health and surveillance implications

The findings have three practical implications. First, surveillance should not use density as the sole indicator of economic sensitivity. Monitoring the growth rate of common-city mapped populations, absolute programme burden, home-versus-street balance, intermediary organisation, cellphone use, and mobility would provide a more informative picture. A stable density can conceal a large expansion in both the city and the population requiring services.

Second, downturn responses should anticipate movement into less visible and more dispersed forms of work. A shift toward home-based or intermediary-mediated arrangements can reduce the reach of interventions designed around identifiable street locations. Programme planning therefore needs flexible outreach through community networks, phones, peers, and intermediaries, while guarding against coercion and gatekeeping. The Pakistani surveillance literature already shows substantial heterogeneity in organisation and vulnerability between cities [@Emmanuel2013Organisation; @Mishra2013Vulnerabilities].

Third, recovery alone should not be expected to reverse the accumulated population. Policies that reduce entry pressure—income protection, employment access, food and housing security—need to be paired with credible exit options for those who want them. The comparative literature indicates that outside employment, safety, stable housing, and continuing income matter to sustained exit, while temporary improvements without viable alternatives can be followed by return [@Ingabire2012Kigali; @Learmonth2015Barriers; @White2020Interruptions; @MahadeshwarZhou2024Outside]. Services should remain available regardless of whether a person continues, pauses, exits, or re-enters sex work.

## Strengths and limitations

This study's principal strength is that it assembles the totality of the published Pakistani data available for this question rather than selecting a single survey round. It preserves the true grain of each source, uses common-city panels for longitudinal comparisons, distinguishes the primary density analysis from secondary and exploratory comparisons, and evaluates multiple markers of population scale, change, and organisation. National indicators were analysed only at the survey-round level, avoiding false precision from repeating one national value across city rows. The complete analysis also includes permutation inference, bootstrap intervals, leave-one-city-out checks, multiplicity adjustment, nonlinear-model comparisons, and executable validation.

Several limitations define what can be concluded. Mapping estimates are programmatic estimates of a hidden population, not censuses, and changes in mapping effort, network reach, category definitions, mobility, and visibility may contribute to differences between rounds [@Emmanuel2013SGS; @Fearon2020ZimbabwePSE]. The Pakistani repeated-surveillance literature itself notes that improved mapping may explain part of the observed growth [@Mishra2013Vulnerabilities]. Survey cities are not a probability sample of all Pakistani cities, and city outcomes were sometimes paired with district urban-stratum measures. Only 2011 published comparable city density, only three rounds contained usable typology measures, the 2014 comparison covered four Punjab cities, and the 2016-17 report contains an unresolved discrepancy between its printed city rows and grand total. These constraints prevent a conventional high-powered national panel analysis.

The statistical results must therefore be read at their appropriate level. The primary density estimate was imprecise and did not provide evidence of a positive city-level association between recent deterioration and FSW density. Only one exploratory cross-sectional association survived false-discovery-rate adjustment, and none of the 32 round-level macro-marker correlations was statistically distinguishable with only three or four observations. City-level correlations between change in household distress and change in mapped counts also varied in direction. These results rule out a simple, stable, city-ranking relationship; they do not erase the directly observed differences in continuous-panel growth rates or organisational composition. Because these surveys are the complete published Pakistani series, the longitudinal contrasts are the evidence available—not a sample waiting to be confirmed by a larger hidden national dataset—but their descriptive coherence should not be mislabelled as individual-level causation.

Finally, the study does not observe client spending, prices, earnings, entry, exit, or re-entry. It therefore cannot separate supply from demand or prove that fewer newcomers caused the recovery plateau. The literature makes this mechanism plausible and explains the observed pattern, but a future longitudinal design would need individual transitions, stable denominators, and measures of mapping intensity and client demand to identify it directly.

## Conclusion

Across the complete available Pakistani evidence, worsening economic conditions coincided with rapid expansion of the mapped FSW population and a shift in how sex work was organised. Improving conditions coincided with a sharp decline in population growth toward a plateau, not an immediate reduction in the accumulated population. The literature shows why these findings belong together: economic stress can increase entry or return and push activity into less visible forms; recovery can improve outside options and reduce net entry, while barriers to exit and renewed client demand preserve the existing population. The most defensible conclusion is therefore that Pakistan's economic signal lies in FSW population dynamics and organisation—not in a single cross-sectional density coefficient. This is a meaningful descriptive relationship in the total available national evidence, while the specific causal flows remain to be measured.
